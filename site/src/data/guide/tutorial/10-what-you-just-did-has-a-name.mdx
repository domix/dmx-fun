Nine chapters without a word of theory, on purpose. You now have working intuitions for every
operation in this library, and intuitions are what the vocabulary is for. The names below do
not add behaviour; they let you recognise the same shape in another library, another language,
or a paper, and they let you read the rest of the literature without a dictionary.

## `map`: a functor

In chapter 2 you wrote `.map(_ -> catalog.save(...))` on a `Result`, and in chapter 3
`.map(percent -> fee - fee * percent / 100)` on an `Option`. Same operation, different box:
apply a plain function to the value inside, leave the box alone. A type that offers `map` with
two obvious rules, mapping the identity changes nothing and mapping `f` then `g` equals mapping
`g ∘ f`, is called a **functor**. `Result`, `Option`, `Try`, `Either`, `Validated`, `Lazy` and
`NonEmptyList` all are. When you see `map` on a new type, you already know what it does.

## `flatMap`: a monad

Chapter 2's chain was five `flatMap`s. Each step returned a new `Result`, and `flatMap` fed the
value forward while letting a failure skip the rest. A functor whose steps can themselves return
the box, with a way to put a plain value into the box (`Result.ok`, `Option.some`) and a
`flatMap` that does not nest boxes, is a **monad**. The "two tracks" picture from chapter 2 is
the monad for `Result`; the "skip to `getOrElse`" picture from chapter 3 is the monad for
`Option`. Sequential, dependent steps: that is the whole idea.

## `combine`: an applicative

Chapter 5 was the one place where `flatMap` was the wrong tool. Three independent field checks
had to run regardless of each other and report every failure. `Validated.combine`, and `Guard.allOf`
on top of it, run the checks side by side and merge the outcomes. A type that can combine
independent computations this way is an **applicative**. The practical distinction from chapter
5 is the whole theory: dependent steps compose with `flatMap` and stop at the first failure;
independent checks compose with `combine` and accumulate. `Validated` deliberately has no
useful `flatMap` across errors, because that would quietly turn accumulation back into
short-circuiting.

## Everything else, in one line each

- `recoverWith` and `mapError` (chapters 2 and 5): the same two operations, on the other track.
- `Either.fold` (chapter 6): collapse both sides into one value; the pattern-matching `switch` is
  the same thing with the compiler watching.
- `Option.toResult`, `Try.toResult`, `Validated.toResult` (chapters 3, 4, 5): the bridges. Every
  type here converts to its neighbours, and the conversions are where you choose which failures
  become which.
- `Lazy` (chapter 8): a functor and a monad over "not computed yet".
- `NonEmptyList` (chapter 7): a functor whose extra guarantee is a size.
- Not in this tutorial: `Tuple2..4`, `Accumulator` and the `Pipelines` helpers. They are small and
  the reference pages explain them in a screen each.

## What you do with the names

Nothing new in your code. Use them to read: Vavr, Arrow, cats and fp-ts use the same words for
the same shapes, and the <a href={`${base}blog/vavr-arrow-cats-fp-ts-what-problems-they-solve`}>comparison post</a>
maps them to dmx-fun. Use them to explain: "this is a `flatMap` chain" is shorter than chapter 2.
And use them as a smell test: if a type in your codebase has `map` but the two rules above do not
hold, something is wrong with it.
